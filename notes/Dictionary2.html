Compressed Dictionary Methods for Inverse Problems

Cluster → Compress → Select → Solve: making huge dictionaries tractable

The Problem

In applications like MEG source localization, MRI fingerprinting, and hyperspectral imaging, we model observed data as a linear combination of "atoms" from a dictionary:

Forward model $$\mathbf{b} = D\mathbf{x} + \boldsymbol{\epsilon}$$

where \(D \in \mathbb{R}^{n \times p}\) is the dictionary (each column \(\mathbf{d}_j\) is an atom), \(\mathbf{x} \in \mathbb{R}^{p}\) is a sparse coefficient vector, and \(\boldsymbol{\epsilon}\) is noise. The problem: \(p\) can be enormous (hundreds of thousands of atoms), making standard sparse solvers too expensive. We need a way to compress the dictionary without throwing away important structure.

Step 1

Partition the Dictionary into Clusters

Split the \(p\) columns of \(D\) into \(L\) groups of similar atoms:

$$D = \begin{bmatrix} D^{(1)} & D^{(2)} & \cdots & D^{(L)} \end{bmatrix}$$

where \(D^{(\ell)} \in \mathbb{R}^{n \times p_\ell}\) and the group sizes sum to the total: \(\sum_{\ell=1}^{L} p_\ell = p\).

Clustering can be supervised (e.g., group dipoles by brain region in MEG) or unsupervised (k-means or k-medoids on the columns of \(D\)).

Atoms in the same cluster point in roughly the same direction in \(\mathbb{R}^n\). That means each cluster \(D^{(\ell)}\) lives near a low-dimensional subspace — which is exactly what we'll exploit next.
Step 2

Low-Rank Compression of Each Cluster

Because similar atoms span a low-dimensional subspace, we approximate each cluster with a rank-\(k_\ell\) factorization:

Low-rank decomposition $$D^{(\ell)} = W^{(\ell)} H^{(\ell)} + E^{(\ell)}$$

where:

\(W^{(\ell)} \in \mathbb{R}^{n \times k_\ell}\) — the basis for the subspace (like principal components)
\(H^{(\ell)} \in \mathbb{R}^{k_\ell \times p_\ell}\) — the coefficients of each atom in that basis
\(E^{(\ell)} \in \mathbb{R}^{n \times p_\ell}\) — the approximation error
\(k_\ell \ll p_\ell\) — huge compression

In practice, this factorization comes from truncated SVD (equivalently PCA) of \(D^{(\ell)}\). Keep the top \(k_\ell\) singular vectors.

Instead of storing all \(p_\ell\) atoms (columns), we store just \(k_\ell\) basis vectors. Each original atom is approximately a short linear combination of these basis vectors. This is just PCA applied to each cluster.
Step 3

Assemble the Global Compressed Dictionary

Stack all the cluster bases side by side, and arrange the coefficient matrices block-diagonally:

Block structure $$D \;\approx\; \underbrace{\begin{bmatrix} W^{(1)} & \cdots & W^{(L)} \end{bmatrix}}_{W} \;\underbrace{\begin{bmatrix} H^{(1)} & & \\ & \ddots & \\ & & H^{(L)} \end{bmatrix}}_{H}$$

So the full dictionary factors as:

$$D = WH + E$$

where \(W \in \mathbb{R}^{n \times K}\) with \(K = \sum k_\ell \ll p\), and \(H\) is block-diagonal. The original problem \(\mathbf{b} \approx D\mathbf{x}\) becomes \(\mathbf{b} \approx W(H\mathbf{x})\). If we define \(\mathbf{h} = H\mathbf{x}\), we're solving in a much smaller \(K\)-dimensional space.

Step 4

Model the Compression Error Statistically

The compression isn't perfect. Each atom has a residual:

$$\mathbf{d}_j = W\mathbf{h}_j + \mathbf{e}_j$$

The key insight: don't ignore these errors — model them as a random variable. Collect all \(p\) error vectors and compute their sample statistics:

Error statistics $$\boldsymbol{\mu}_{\text{DCE}} = \frac{1}{p}\sum_{j=1}^{p}\mathbf{e}_j$$
Error covariance $$C_{\text{DCE}} = \frac{1}{p-1}\sum_{j=1}^{p}(\mathbf{e}_j - \boldsymbol{\mu}_{\text{DCE}})(\mathbf{e}_j - \boldsymbol{\mu}_{\text{DCE}})^\top + \epsilon I$$

The \(\epsilon I\) term is Tikhonov regularization — it ensures \(C_{\text{DCE}}\) is invertible even when \(n > p\).

We then assume a Gaussian model for the error:

$$\mathbf{e} \sim \mathcal{N}\!\left(\boldsymbol{\mu}_{\text{DCE}},\; C_{\text{DCE}}\right)$$
The compression error isn't just white noise — it has structure (direction, spread). By estimating its covariance, we can "correct" for the compression when solving. This is the same idea as generalized least squares in econometrics.

The Resulting Likelihood

Instead of minimizing a plain squared error, we get a covariance-weighted objective. The likelihood of observing \(\mathbf{b}\) given coefficients \(\mathbf{h}\) is:

Gaussian likelihood $$p(\mathbf{b}\mid\mathbf{h}) \propto \exp\!\left(-\tfrac{1}{2}(\mathbf{b} - W\mathbf{h} - \boldsymbol{\mu}_{\text{DCE}})^\top C_{\text{DCE}}^{-1}(\mathbf{b} - W\mathbf{h} - \boldsymbol{\mu}_{\text{DCE}})\right)$$

Maximizing this likelihood (equivalently, minimizing the negative log) gives the objective:

Mahalanobis objective $$\min_{\mathbf{h}} \;\left\|\mathbf{b} - W\mathbf{h} - \boldsymbol{\mu}_{\text{DCE}}\right\|_{C_{\text{DCE}}^{-1}}^{2}$$

Naïve (ignore error)

$$\min_{\mathbf{h}} \|\mathbf{b} - W\mathbf{h}\|_2^2$$

Treats all directions equally. Biased by systematic compression error.

Covariance-corrected

$$\min_{\mathbf{h}} \|\mathbf{b} - W\mathbf{h}\|_{C^{-1}}^2$$

Down-weights directions where compression error is large. Unbiased.

Step 5

Select Active Clusters (Group Sparsity)

Not all \(L\) clusters are needed. We assume only a few clusters \(j_1, \dots, j_r\) (with \(r \ll L\)) are active:

Sparse cluster selection $$\mathbf{b} \approx W^{(j_1)}\mathbf{h}^{(j_1)} + W^{(j_2)}\mathbf{h}^{(j_2)} + \cdots + W^{(j_r)}\mathbf{h}^{(j_r)} + \boldsymbol{\epsilon}'$$

This is group sparsity: entire clusters are either "on" or "off." Selection can be done greedily (like group OMP) or via group Lasso-type penalties.

In MEG, this means: out of 50 brain regions, maybe only 3 are active during a task. We find those 3 in the compressed space cheaply, then refine.
Step 6

Solve in Original Space (Selected Clusters Only)

Having identified the \(r\) active clusters, go back to the original atoms in those clusters and solve the full-resolution problem:

Final reconstruction $$\mathbf{b} = D^{(j_1)}\mathbf{x}^{(j_1)} + \cdots + D^{(j_r)}\mathbf{x}^{(j_r)} + \tilde{\boldsymbol{\epsilon}}$$

Now you only solve over \(p_{j_1} + \cdots + p_{j_r}\) atoms instead of all \(p\). This is a much smaller problem — standard sparse solvers (OMP, LASSO, etc.) work fine here.

Putting It All Together

The full pipeline in equations:

$$\begin{aligned} &\textbf{1. Partition:} & D &= \begin{bmatrix} D^{(1)} & \cdots & D^{(L)} \end{bmatrix} \\[6pt] &\textbf{2. Compress:} & D^{(\ell)} &\approx W^{(\ell)}H^{(\ell)} \quad \text{via truncated SVD} \\[6pt] &\textbf{3. Stack:} & D &\approx WH, \quad W \in \mathbb{R}^{n \times K},\; K \ll p \\[6pt] &\textbf{4. Error model:} & \mathbf{e} &\sim \mathcal{N}(\boldsymbol{\mu}_{\text{DCE}},\, C_{\text{DCE}}) \\[6pt] &\textbf{5. Select:} & \mathbf{b} &\approx \sum_{i=1}^{r} W^{(j_i)}\mathbf{h}^{(j_i)} \quad (r \ll L) \\[6pt] &\textbf{6. Solve:} & \mathbf{b} &\approx \sum_{i=1}^{r} D^{(j_i)}\mathbf{x}^{(j_i)} \quad \text{(full resolution, small problem)} \end{aligned}$$

The method combines four powerful ideas: clustering (group similar atoms), low-rank approximation (compress each group via SVD), statistical error modeling (Gaussian model of compression residuals → Mahalanobis-weighted fitting), and group sparsity (select only relevant clusters). The result: solving a problem with \(p = 100{,}000\) atoms at the cost of working with \(K \approx 500\) compressed dimensions — orders of magnitude faster, with principled error correction.
