Low-Rank Compression of Each Cluster

Clarifying the decomposition, the assumptions, and where it can go wrong

1 Setup: What is D(ℓ)?

Start with your full dictionary \( D \in \mathbb{R}^{n \times p} \), where each column is an atom (a signal template). After clustering the atoms into groups, \( D^{(\ell)} \) is simply the sub-matrix containing only the atoms assigned to cluster \( \ell \).

Sub-dictionary for cluster ℓ \( D^{(\ell)} \in \mathbb{R}^{n \times p_\ell} \)

Here \( n \) is the atom dimension (signal length) and \( p_\ell \) is the number of atoms in cluster \( \ell \).

2 The Decomposition

We approximate this sub-dictionary with a low-rank factorization:

Low-rank approximation \( D^{(\ell)} \;=\; W^{(\ell)}\, H^{(\ell)} \;+\; E^{(\ell)} \)
\( W^{(\ell)} \in \mathbb{R}^{n \times k_\ell} \) Basis matrix. The \( k_\ell \) principal directions (left singular vectors) that span the subspace.
\( H^{(\ell)} \in \mathbb{R}^{k_\ell \times p_\ell} \) Coefficient matrix. Each column tells you how to reconstruct one atom from the basis.
\( E^{(\ell)} \in \mathbb{R}^{n \times p_\ell} \) Error matrix. What the rank-\( k_\ell \) approximation fails to capture.
\( k_\ell \ll p_\ell \) Compression condition. Far fewer basis vectors than atoms — this is what gives the savings.

3 Where It Comes From: Truncated SVD

Compute the full SVD of the sub-dictionary:

Full SVD \( D^{(\ell)} = U \, \Sigma \, V^\top \)

Then truncate — keep only the top \( k_\ell \) singular values and their corresponding vectors:

Truncated SVD \( D^{(\ell)} \;\approx\; \underbrace{U_{k_\ell}}_{\displaystyle W^{(\ell)}} \;\; \underbrace{\Sigma_{k_\ell} \, V_{k_\ell}^\top}_{\displaystyle H^{(\ell)}} \)
The Eckart–Young theorem guarantees this is the best rank-\( k_\ell \) approximation in both Frobenius and spectral norms. You can't do better with any other factorization.

4 Why It Compresses

Before (raw storage)

\( n \cdot p_\ell \) Store every atom in full

After (factored storage)

\( k_\ell (n + p_\ell) \) Store \( W \) and \( H \) only

The savings ratio is \(\; \dfrac{k_\ell(n + p_\ell)}{n \cdot p_\ell} \;\). This is a win when \( k_\ell \) is much smaller than both \( n \) and \( p_\ell \). For example, if \( n = 1000 \), \( p_\ell = 500 \), and \( k_\ell = 10 \):

Raw

500,000 entries

Compressed

15,000 entries — a 33× reduction

5 The Key Assumption (and When It Fails)

This whole approach rests on one assumption: atoms within a cluster are similar enough to lie near a low-dimensional subspace. If you clustered well, similar atoms point in roughly the same directions in \( \mathbb{R}^n \), so a few principal components capture most of the variance.

If clustering is poor — atoms within a group are actually diverse — the singular values will decay slowly, \( k_\ell \) must be large, and compression vanishes.

6 PCA vs. SVD: A Subtle Mismatch

The original text says this is "equivalently PCA". That's slightly sloppy. Here's the difference:

Truncated SVD Factorizes \( D^{(\ell)} \) directly. No centering.
PCA Subtracts the column mean \( \bar{d} \) first, then does SVD on the centered matrix \( D^{(\ell)} - \bar{d}\,\mathbf{1}^\top \).
They're equivalent only if the atoms within each cluster are already zero-mean. In general, they are not — the cluster centroid is nonzero. Using raw SVD without centering wastes one singular vector capturing the mean direction instead of actual variation.

A cleaner approach: subtract the cluster centroid, do truncated SVD on the residuals, and store the centroid separately.

Summary — Cluster → center → truncated SVD → store (centroid + basis + coefficients) → reconstruct on demand.
